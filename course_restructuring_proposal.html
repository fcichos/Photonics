    Introduction to Photonics — Restructured Course (15 Lectures)

    Overview

    The restructured course condenses the theoretical foundations into two introductory lectures, then builds a strong block on anisotropic materials, polarization, and nonlinear optics. The central part of the course develops Fourier optics as the unifying framework for understanding microscopy and imaging. The course closes with modern applications and scattering in random media.

    Part I: Foundations of Light (Lectures 1–2)

    Lecture 1 — Theories of Light: From Rays to Waves

    Existing material: lecture02/02-lecture02.qmd, lecture02/01-lecture02.qmd

    • Historical overview: corpuscular → wave → electromagnetic theory
    • Ray optics essentials: Fermat’s principle, Snell’s law, matrix optics (ABCD matrices)
    • Wave optics postulates: wave equation, monochromatic waves, complex amplitude
    • Plane waves, spherical waves, Gaussian beams (preview)
    • Intensity, wavefronts, dispersion relation
    • Propagation in media, refractive index

    Lecture 2 — Electromagnetic and Quantum Theory of Light

    Existing material: lecture10/01-lecture10.qmd (quantum optics), lecture10/EM Waves in Matter.qmd

    • Maxwell’s equations recap: EM waves, Poynting vector, energy density
    • EM waves in matter: polarization of matter, susceptibility, dielectric function
    • Fresnel equations and reflection/refraction at interfaces
    • Brief introduction to quantization of the EM field
    • Photon concept: Fock states, coherent states
    • Single-photon sources, photon statistics (Poissonian vs. sub-Poissonian)
    • Hong-Ou-Mandel effect as a quantum signature (demonstration, not derivation-heavy)

    Part II: Anisotropic Media, Polarization & Nonlinear Optics (Lectures 3–5)

    Lecture 3 — Polarization of Light

    Partially new, draws from lecture11/anisotropic_media.qmd

    • Polarization states: linear, circular, elliptical
    • Jones vectors and Jones matrices
    • Stokes parameters and Müller matrices
    • Polarization measurement techniques
    • Polarizing elements: polarizers, waveplates (λ/2, λ/4), rotators

    Lecture 4 — Light Propagation in Anisotropic Materials

    Existing material: lecture11/anisotropic_media.qmd, lecture12/anisotropic_media.qmd

    • Permittivity tensor and principal axes
    • Wave equation in anisotropic media
    • Ordinary and extraordinary rays
    • Birefringence: uniaxial and biaxial crystals
    • Fresnel’s wave surface equation
    • Index ellipsoid and wave-vector surface
    • Applications: waveplates, compensators, polarizing beam splitters

    Lecture 5 — Nonlinear Optics

    New lecture

    • Origin of optical nonlinearity: anharmonic oscillator model
    • Nonlinear susceptibility χ⁽²⁾ and χ⁽³⁾
    • Second-harmonic generation (SHG)
    • Phase matching: angle tuning, temperature tuning, quasi-phase matching
    • Sum/difference frequency generation, optical parametric amplification
    • Third-order effects: self-phase modulation, Kerr effect, four-wave mixing
    • Applications: frequency conversion, ultrafast pulse generation, nonlinear microscopy

    Part III: Fourier Optics & Microscopy (Lectures 6–11)

    Lecture 6 — Foundations of Fourier Optics

    Existing material: lecture05/01-lecture05.qmd, lecture05/lecture05-supplements.qmd

    • Fourier transform review: definitions, properties, important pairs
    • Spatial frequencies and angular spectrum representation
    • Propagation of light as a spatial frequency filter
    • Free-space propagation: Fresnel and Fraunhofer diffraction as Fourier transforms
    • The lens as a Fourier transformer

    Lecture 7 — Diffraction and Optical Transfer

    Partially existing, partially new

    • Diffraction by apertures: single slit, circular aperture, gratings
    • Airy pattern and the Rayleigh criterion
    • Point spread function (PSF) and optical transfer function (OTF)
    • Modulation transfer function (MTF)
    • Coherent vs. incoherent transfer functions
    • Resolution limits: Abbe diffraction limit

    Lecture 8 — Imaging Theory and Microscopy I: Brightfield and Darkfield

    New lecture

    • Image formation as a double Fourier transform (Abbe theory)
    • Köhler illumination and coherence in microscopy
    • Brightfield microscopy: contrast mechanisms, limitations for phase objects
    • Fourier filtering in the back focal plane
    • Darkfield microscopy: blocking the zero order
    • Oblique illumination and Schlieren methods
    • Practical resolution: numerical aperture, immersion

    Lecture 9 — Microscopy II: Phase Contrast and Fluorescence

    New lecture

    • The phase problem: transparent specimens produce no amplitude contrast
    • Zernike phase contrast microscopy: λ/4 ring in Fourier plane
    • Differential interference contrast (DIC / Nomarski)
    • Fluorescence microscopy fundamentals: Stokes shift, filters, dichroics
    • Widefield fluorescence imaging: PSF, out-of-focus blur
    • Confocal laser scanning microscopy: point scanning, optical sectioning
    • Confocal PSF and resolution improvement

    Lecture 10 — Microscopy III: Advanced Techniques via Fourier Optics

    New lecture

    • Structured illumination: Moiré effect, frequency mixing, resolution doubling
    • Light sheet microscopy: decoupling illumination and detection
    • Optical coherence tomography (OCT): low-coherence interferometry, axial sectioning
    • Ptychography: phase retrieval from intensity measurements
    • Holographic microscopy: off-axis holography, digital reconstruction

    Lecture 11 — Wavefront Sensing and Adaptive Optics

    New lecture

    • Wavefront aberrations: Zernike polynomials
    • Shack-Hartmann wavefront sensor: principle, microlens arrays, centroid detection
    • Wavefront reconstruction algorithms
    • Adaptive optics: deformable mirrors, closed-loop correction
    • Applications in astronomy, ophthalmology, and microscopy
    • Computational wavefront sensing: transport of intensity equation

    Part IV: Modern Applications (Lectures 12–13)

    Lecture 12 — Superresolution Microscopy

    New lecture

    • Breaking the diffraction limit: why and how
    • Single-molecule localization microscopy (PALM/STORM)
      • Photoswitchable fluorophores, localization precision
      • Connection to PSF and Fourier optics
    • Stimulated emission depletion (STED)
      • PSF engineering, doughnut beam
    • Structured illumination microscopy (SIM) — expanded from Lecture 10
    • MINFLUX: minimal photon fluxes for nanometer localization
    • Comparison of methods: resolution, speed, live-cell capability

    Lecture 13 — Photothermal Detection and Sensing

    New lecture

    • Light absorption and heat generation at the nanoscale
    • Photothermal effect: refractive index changes, thermal lensing
    • Photothermal microscopy: pump-probe scheme, lock-in detection
    • Photothermal single-particle detection: gold nanoparticles, single molecules
    • Photothermal heterodyne imaging
    • Applications: label-free imaging, nanoparticle tracking, biosensing
    • Connection to photoacoustic imaging

    Part V: Scattering and Random Media (Lectures 14–15)

    Lecture 14 — Light Scattering: Fundamentals

    New lecture

    • Rayleigh scattering: small particles, wavelength dependence, polarization
    • Mie scattering: larger particles, scattering cross sections, form factor
    • Scattering matrix and Mueller matrix for single particles
    • Dynamic light scattering (DLS): autocorrelation, hydrodynamic radius
    • Static light scattering: form factor, structure factor
    • Scattering by ensembles: turbidity, extinction

    Lecture 15 — Light Transport in Random Media

    New lecture

    • Multiple scattering: from single scattering to diffusion
    • Transport mean free path and scattering mean free path
    • Radiative transfer equation (qualitative)
    • Diffusion approximation for light transport
    • Speckle patterns: statistics, correlations, memory effect
    • Diffusing wave spectroscopy (DWS)
    • Wavefront shaping through scattering media: transmission matrix, focusing through opaque media
    • Anderson localization of light (brief outlook)

    Notes

    • Lectures 1–2 group what was previously 6 separate lectures (ray optics through quantum) into two sessions. Ray optics / matrix optics stays concise; quantum optics is presented as a conceptual overview rather than full formalism.
    • The anisotropic/polarization block (L3–4) uses existing material from lectures 7, 11, 12 but adds a dedicated polarization lecture (Jones/Müller calculus).
    • Fourier optics (L6–7) keeps existing content and becomes the language for all subsequent microscopy lectures (L8–10).
    • The microscopy block (L8–10) is entirely new and uses Fourier optics as its unifying framework.
    • The applications block (L11–13) connects naturally to the Fourier/microscopy framework.
    • Scattering (L14–15) provides a strong conclusion, connecting back to EM theory and extending into modern research topics.
