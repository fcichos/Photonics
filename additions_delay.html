    Experimental papers worth adding, with suggested insertion points

    I’ve focused on papers that (a) genuinely fill gaps I flagged, (b) postdate or sit alongside what you already cite, and (c) are real, verified, and on-topic. Five additions, in order of how strongly I’d recommend including each.

    1. Lee et al., MegaVanderTest — 100 CAVs on a real highway (Sec. IV D)

    Reference: Lee, J. W. et al., “Traffic Control via Connected and Automated Vehicles (CAVs): An Open-Road Field Experiment with 100 CAVs,” IEEE Control Systems Magazine 45, 2025 (doi:10.1109/MCS.2024.3498552; arXiv:2402.17043).

    Why it fits: This is the largest open-road CAV field experiment to date, designed explicitly to dampen stop-and-go waves — i.e., the “phantom traffic jams” you discuss in Sec. IV D. It is the closest current real-world counterpart to the Sugiyama et al. circular-track experiment (your ref. 391), and it directly validates the picture that delays drive instability and that controllers can suppress them.

    Suggested location: End of the paragraph that introduces Fig. 15 (page 23), just before the OVM equations. Add as a new sentence after “…further contribute to traffic flow instabilities \(^{412}\), and, together with finite braking distances, to accidents \(^{351,420,421}\).”

    Suggested wording (in your style):

    Recently, a large-scale open-road field experiment with 100 longitudinally controlled vehicles deployed on a public highway demonstrated that hierarchical control strategies can systematically dampen stop-and-go waves in mixed-autonomy traffic [new ref. Lee 2025], providing a real-world counterpart to the circular-track experiment of Ref.\(^{391}\) and confirming the role of reaction- and communication-time delays in macroscopic traffic instabilities.

    2. Tapia García et al. (or similar) — outdoor drone-swarm phase transition (Sec. IV C 2)

    Reference: “Flocking phase transition and threat responses in bio-inspired autonomous drone swarms,” arXiv:2512.21196 (December 2025) — outdoor experiments with a 10-drone swarm mapping a flocking phase diagram and showing operation near the transition enhances responsiveness.

    Why it fits: You explicitly note that “to the best of our knowledge, the effects of retarded interactions in these extensions of the VM have not yet been studied” — but this 2025 outdoor experiment maps a phase diagram for a real flocking swarm and discusses delays and noise as destabilizers, which is the closest available experimental counterpart to your delayed Vicsek phase diagrams (Fig. 13). Note: this is recent (Dec 2025) and you should check the published version, since it may still be a preprint at the time of submission.

    Suggested location: Sec. IV C 2, in the paragraph after Fig. 13, where you discuss order–disorder transitions in variants of the VM. Add a sentence acknowledging recent outdoor drone experiments that begin to probe the predicted phase boundaries.

    Suggested wording:

    Recent outdoor experiments with autonomous drone swarms have started to map flocking phase diagrams under real-world sensing noise and communication delays [new ref.], providing a first experimental window onto the order–disorder transition explored theoretically in the delayed Vicsek model. A systematic experimental scan of delay as a control parameter, along the lines of Fig. 13, remains an open challenge.

    (If the arXiv preprint hasn’t appeared in a peer-reviewed journal by your submission date, I’d cite it as arXiv only or hold it for a revision.)

    3. Heuthe, Iyer, Gompper & Bechinger — colloidal swarmalators (Sec. IV A 2)

    Reference: Heuthe, V.-L., Iyer, P., Gompper, G. & Bechinger, C. “Tunable colloidal swarmalators with hydrodynamic coupling,” Nature Communications 16, 10984 (2025). doi:10.1038/s41467-025-66830-5.

    Why it fits: This is already your ref. 330, but it’s cited only in a single sentence (“In Ref.\(^{330}\), the authors employed a similar system as an experimental realization of swarmalators…”). The 2025 Nature Communications paper is the formal published version (your current citation appears to be to the preprint). Two things are worth doing:

    • Update the citation to the Nature Communications version with the DOI above.
    • The published paper is explicit that the orbital motion of each colloid is induced by a small feedback time delay (“Each particle aims to move towards a reference point guided by a laser-based feedback loop, but with a small time-delay which induces an orbiting, oscillatory motion”). This is a textbook illustration of delay-induced collective behavior and deserves a sentence rather than a single half-line.

    Suggested expansion (replacing your current single sentence on Ref. 330):

    In Ref.\(^{330}\), the authors employed a similar system as an experimental realization of swarmalators—agents that move chaotically in space yet synchronize in time. Here the orbital motion of each colloid around its reference point is itself a direct consequence of the feedback delay, and tuning a single coupling parameter switches the collective between synchronized clusters, rotating swarmalator states, and dispersed configurations. The individual rotators subsequently synchronize through hydrodynamic interactions.

    4. Tenenbaum et al. (vibrobots) — inertia-tuned clustering (Sec. IV B)

    Reference: “Dynamical clustering and wetting phenomena in inertial active matter,” Communications Physics 7, 343 (2024). doi:10.1038/s42005-024-01835-y.

    Why it fits: Your Sec. IV B is thin on recent experiments — you cite Scholz et al. 2018 (ref. 44) and a few macroscale realizations. This 2024 vibrobot paper experimentally tunes inertia (via shaker frequency) and observes (i) suppression of motility-induced clustering by inertia, (ii) an inertia-driven solid–liquid transition in cluster interiors, and (iii) annihilation of wetting at boundaries. This is directly relevant to the MIPS-with-inertia discussion at the end of Sec. IV B, which currently cites only theory/simulation (refs. 24, 73, 336, 347–349).

    Suggested location: In Sec. IV B, in the “Motility-Induced Phase Separation” paragraph, after the sentence “Third, inertia reduces the growth exponent of dense clusters compared to the over-damped case…”

    Suggested wording:

    These theoretical predictions have recently been confirmed in vibrobot experiments where inertia is systematically tuned via the shaker excitation frequency [new ref. Comm. Phys. 2024]. The experiments reveal not only the predicted suppression of clustering but also an inertia-induced solid-to-liquid transition within clusters and a complete annihilation of wetting phenomena at confining boundaries, both absent in overdamped active Brownian motion.

    One structural recommendation

    In Sec. V, before the bullet list of open questions, I’d add a short paragraph that explicitly takes stock of the experimental state of the field — something like:

    Experimental landscape. The experimental study of delay in active matter is markedly asymmetric across scales. At the microscopic end, feedback-controlled colloids and active Brownian particle realizations allow direct and tunable delays, and have provided quantitative tests of single-particle and few-body predictions \(^{12,39,43,151,258,330}\). At the macroscopic end, large-scale field experiments with autonomous vehicles \(^{\text{CIRCLES}}\) and drones \(^{11,\,\text{new drone ref.}}\) have only recently begun to probe the role of delay in genuine many-body collective dynamics, while controlled experiments on natural flocks with delay as a tunable parameter remain out of reach. The mesoscopic regime — vibrobots, robotic swarms, mixed-reality setups \(^{42,44,45}\) — currently offers the most direct bridge between theory and experiment in many-body delay phenomena, but a systematic experimental scan of delay across the predicted phase boundaries of the delayed Vicsek and Cucker–Smale families is still missing.

    This paragraph (a) honestly maps experiment to theory, (b) flags that experiment is well ahead at the single-particle level and well behind at the flock level, and (c) makes your bullet “Experimental validation” land harder because it follows a concrete diagnosis rather than abstract hand-waving.
